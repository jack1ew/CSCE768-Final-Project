                                                                 Abstract
                          This study investigates how win prediction accuracy evolves as Counter-Strike: Global Of-
                       fensive (CS:GO) matches progress from round 1 to round 30, with particular emphasis on
                       economic momentum patterns and their impact on match outcomes. Using three large-scale
                       datasets comprising 1.7 million samples across 33,268 matches, we train and evaluate five ma-
                       chine learning models (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, k-Nearest Neighbors, Multi-Layer
                       Perceptron, and Transformer Encoder) to predict match winners at various stages of gameplay.
                       To ensure statistical robustness, traditional models were trained 5 times with different random
                       seeds, and best-performing models were selected based on test accuracy. Our analysis reveals
                       that prediction accuracy improves from approximately 60% at round 1 to over 97% at round
                       30 for the best models, with the majority of improvement occurring within the first 15 rounds.
                       Wefurther demonstrate that economic factors—specifically equipment value, forced eco rounds,
                       and equipment savings—create cascading advantages that compound score differentials. Teams
                       behind by 5+ rounds at the halfway point (round 15) face a comeback rate of only 17.6%, which
                       drops to 14.2% when economically disadvantaged. The Transformer model achieves the high-
                       est performance with 97.8% accuracy on the ESTA dataset (99.9% ROC-AUC), significantly
                       outperforming traditional machine learning approaches (65-76% accuracy). Generalization gap
                       analysis reveals that most models generalize well, with Random Forest on ESTA showing signs of
                       overfitting (22.6% gap). Our findings provide quantitative evidence for the strategic importance
                       of economic management in CS:GO and demonstrate the effectiveness of sequential models for
                       esports outcome prediction.

             1   Introduction
             1.1  Background and Motivation
             Counter-Strike: Global Offensive (CS:GO) is a tactical first-person shooter game where two teams
             of five players compete in rounds-based matches. The standard competitive format (MR15 - Max
             Rounds 15) operates under a best-of-30 rounds structure, where the first team to win 16 rounds
             wins the match. Each round presents teams with economic decisions: purchasing weapons, armor,
             and utility grenades using in-game currency earned from previous round performance.
                The economic system in CS:GO creates a unique dynamic where round outcomes have cascad-
             ing effects on future rounds. Teams that win rounds typically save expensive equipment (rifles,
             armor) for subsequent rounds, while losing teams may be forced into ”eco rounds” where they pur-
             chase minimal or no equipment due to insufÏcient funds. This economic momentum can amplify
             advantages and make comebacks increasingly difÏcult.
                Understanding when and why matches become predictable has significant implications for:
                • Competitive Strategy: Teams can make informed decisions about economic management
                  and round-by-round tactics
                • Esports Analytics: Analysts and commentators can provide data-driven insights during
                  live broadcasts
                • Machine Learning Research: CS:GO provides a rich domain for studying sequential
                  decision-making and momentum-based predictions
                • Sports Betting: Accurate probability models enable fair odds-setting in esports wagering
                  markets
             1.2  Research Questions
             This study addresses the following research questions:
               1. RQ1: Does win prediction accuracy improve as CS:GO matches progress from round 1 to
                  round 30, and if so, at what rate?
               2. RQ2: How impactful is winning the first round on overall match outcomes?
               3. RQ3: At what score differential do matches become effectively decided, and what comeback
                  potential exists?
               4. RQ4: How do economic factors (equipment value, forced eco rounds, equipment savings)
                  create and compound momentum?
               5. RQ5: Which game statistics are most important for predicting match outcomes across dif-
                  ferent datasets?
               6. RQ6: How do different machine learning architectures (traditional vs. sequence-based) com-
                  pare for esports outcome prediction?
               7. RQ7: Shouldovertimeroundsbeanalyzedseparatelyfromregulationroundsduetoeconomic
                  reset mechanics?

             2   Related Work
             2.1  Esports Analytics and Outcome Prediction
             Esports analytics has emerged as a growing research area at the intersection of sports analytics,
             machine learning, and game theory. Previous work in this domain can be categorized into several
             key areas:
             2.1.1  Traditional Sports Win Probability Models
             Win probability models have a long history in traditional sports analytics. Lock and Nettleton [1]
             developed in-game win probability models for basketball using logistic regression, demonstrating
             that probability estimates improve as games progress. Similarly, Stern [2] modeled game score
             dynamics using Brownian motion processes.
                These traditional approaches typically assume:
                • Independent possessions or plays
                • Stationary team strength parameters
                • Minimal carryover effects between discrete events
                CS:GO violates these assumptions due to its economic momentum system, where round out-
             comes have cascading effects on future rounds through equipment persistence.
             2.1.2  Esports-Specific Prediction Models
             Recent work has begun addressing esports prediction challenges. Yang et al. [3] predicted match
             outcomes in League of Legends (LoL) using early-game statistics, achieving 74% accuracy using
             RandomForests. Hodgeetal.[4]developedwinprobabilitygraphsforDota2usingneuralnetworks.
                However, these studies focus on match-level prediction (who will win) rather than round-by-
             round evolution of prediction confidence. Our work fills this gap by analyzing how certainty in-
             creases as matches progress.
             2.1.3  CS:GO-Specific Research
             Prior CS:GO research has examined:
                • Economic modeling: Agarwala and Pearce [5] analyzed optimal buying strategies
                • Playerperformance: Xenopoulosetal.[6]createdcomprehensivegameplaydatasets(ESTA)
                • Match outcome prediction: Silva et al. [7] predicted match winners using team statistics
                Our work differs by focusing on within-match dynamics and economic momentum, rather than
             pre-match predictions or isolated economic decisions.
             2.2  Economic Systems in Games
             The CS:GO economic system creates momentum through:
               1. Equipment Persistence: Weapons and utility saved from won rounds carry forward
               2. Loss Bonuses: Losing teams receive increasing bonuses (up to 5 consecutive losses)
                                                                                         Page 6
             CSCE768 Final Project                                                  Jackie Wang
               3. Forced Eco Rounds: Teams must occasionally skip buying to save for future full buys
                This creates a complex strategic landscape where teams must balance short-term round win-
             ning with long-term economic positioning. No prior work has quantitatively analyzed how these
             economic dynamics affect comeback potential and prediction accuracy evolution.
             2.3  Sequential Models for Time-Series Prediction
             Transformer architectures [8], originally developed for natural language processing, have shown
             strong performance on sequential prediction tasks. Recent applications to sports include:
                • NBA play-by-play prediction [9]
                • Soccer event sequence modeling [10]
                • Cricket outcome prediction [11]
                We extend this work to CS:GO, demonstrating that Transformers can effectively model the
             sequential dependencies between rounds, including economic carryover effects.
             2.4  Research Gap
             While previous work has examined match-level prediction and economic modeling in isolation, no
             prior study has:
               1. Analyzed how prediction accuracy evolves round-by-round across an entire match
               2. Quantified the impact of economic momentum on comeback potential
               3. Compared feature importance across datasets with different feature compositions
               4. Proposed treating overtime as independent ”mini-games” due to economic resets
               5. Applied Transformer models to round-by-round CS:GO prediction
                This work addresses these gaps through comprehensive empirical analysis across 1.7 million
             samples.

             3.5  Data Preprocessing
             3.5.1  Train-Test Split
             Weperform match-level splitting to prevent data leakage:
               1. Identify all unique match id values
               2. Randomly assign 80% of matches to training set
               3. Assign remaining 20% to test set
               4. All rounds from a single match belong to the same split
                This ensures the model never sees any rounds from test matches during training, providing a
             realistic evaluation of generalization performance.
             3.5.2  Feature Normalization
             All numerical features are standardized using scikit-learn’s StandardScaler:
                                                x    =x−µ                                   (1)
                                                 scaled  σ
             where µ is the mean and σ is the standard deviation computed on the training set only.
             3.5.3  Missing Value Handling
             Missing values (e.g., utility usage not recorded in Kaggle dataset) are imputed with 0, which is
             appropriate as:
                • Missing utility values indicate feature unavailability, not missing data
                • After standardization, 0 becomes the mean value
                • Random Forests and Tree-based models handle missing values naturally

             4.3  Round-by-Round Analysis Procedure
             To analyze accuracy evolution, we:
               1. Train each model on the full regulation dataset (rounds 1-30)
               2. For each round r ∈ {1,2,...,30}:
                    • Filter test set to rounds ≤ r
                    • Generate predictions using features available at round r
                    • Compute accuracy, log loss, Brier score, ROC-AUC
               3. Plot metrics vs. round number to visualize evolution
                This procedure reveals when matches become predictable and which models improve fastest.
             4.4  Multi-Run Training Protocol
             Toensure statistical robustness and enable generalization assessment, we employ a multi-run train-
             ing protocol:
               1. Each traditional model (Logistic, Random Forest, k-NN, MLP) is trained 5 times with dif-
                  ferent random seeds
               2. Each run uses a different train/test split (same 80/20 ratio, different random state)
               3. We track accuracy, log-loss, ROC-AUC, and generalization gap (train accuracy - test accu-
                  racy) across runs
               4. The best-performing model from each 5-run set is selected for final evaluation
               5. Transformer models are trained once per dataset due to computational constraints
                This protocol enables:
                • Reporting confidence intervals for model performance
                • Identifying potential overfitting through generalization gap analysis
                • Ensuring reproducibility of results

             7   CNN-Transformer Hybrid Architecture: Advanced Sequence
                 Modeling
             To address limitations in the pure Transformer architecture—specifically its lack of positional
             awareness and inability to capture local temporal patterns—we developed and evaluated a hy-
             brid CNN-Transformer model that combines convolutional feature extraction with global attention
             mechanisms. This section presents comprehensive analysis of the hybrid architecture’s design,
             training dynamics, performance characteristics, and stability challenges.
             7.1  Motivation and Architectural Design
             7.1.1  Pure Transformer Limitations
             The pure Transformer encoder (Section 4.5) achieved strong performance (97.8% accuracy on
             ESTA) but suffers from two critical architectural deficiencies:
               1. Lack of Positional Encoding: Neither the pure Transformer nor any baseline model in-
                  cludes explicit positional embeddings. Standard Transformer architectures [8] inject sinusoidal
                  or learned positional encodings to distinguish sequence order. Without these, the model can-
                  not inherently differentiate round 5 from round 25 except via the round num feature itself,
                  which is a weak proxy for round progression semantics.
               2. Inability to Capture Local Patterns: Self-attention mechanisms excel at modeling long-
                  range dependencies but may miss local temporal patterns such as multi-round win streaks,
                  eco-reset cycles, or equipment-saving strategies that span 2-4 consecutive rounds.
             7.1.2  CNN-Transformer Hybrid Architecture
             Thehybridmodel(models/architectures/cnn transformer.py)addressestheselimitationsthrough
             a four-stage pipeline:
               1. 1D Convolutional Feature Extractor: Two stacked Conv1D layers (kernel size 3, 64 out-
                  put channels) with ReLU activations and dropout (0.1) extract local temporal patterns from
                  round sequences. The convolutional layers operate on input shape (batch, feature dim,
                  seq len) with same-padding to preserve sequence length, enabling detection of multi-round
                  momentum patterns.
               2. Projection Layer: A linear transformation maps the 64-dimensional CNN output to 128-
                  dimensional embeddings compatible with the Transformer encoder, serving as a learned in-
                  terface between local and global feature representations.
               3. Transformer Encoder: Standard multi-head self-attention (4 heads, 2 layers, feedforward
                  dimension 256) captures long-range dependencies across rounds, identical to the pure Trans-
                  former configuration.
               4. Classification Head: Mean-pooling over the sequence dimension aggregates round-level
                  representations into a match-level embedding, followed by LayerNorm → Linear(128→64) →
                  ReLU → Dropout → Linear(64→1) to produce match-win logits.
                                                                                        Page 26
               CSCE768 Final Project                                                            Jackie Wang
                  The architecture employs Kaiming initialization [12] for convolutional layers and Xavier initial-
               ization [13] for linear layers to stabilize gradient flow. Maximum sequence length was increased
               from 30 to 75 rounds to accommodate overtime matches without truncation, though this analysis
               focuses on regulation-only data.
               7.2   Training Methodology and Hyperparameters
               Training incorporated several enhancements beyond the pure Transformer:
                  • Learning Rate Warmup: 2-epoch linear warmup from 0 to 1×10−3 prevents early train-
                    ing divergence, particularly critical for the CNN-Transformer’s more complex optimization
                    landscape.
                                                                                 −3         −5
                  • Cosine Annealing Schedule: Learning rate decays from 1×10       to 1×10    over 10 epochs
                    post-warmup following a cosine curve, enabling fine-grained convergence.
                  • Gradient Clipping: Maximum gradient norm of 1.0 mitigates exploding gradients, which
                    occurred frequently in early experiments without clipping.
                  • NaNLossDetection: Explicitchecksduringtraining(line104of train cnn transformer.py)
                    skip batches with NaN losses to prevent complete training collapse.
                  • Dual Split Strategies: Models trained with both random (80/20) and chronological (time-
                    based) splits to assess temporal generalization under evolving meta-game dynamics.
                  All models trained for 12 epochs with batch size 64, BCEWithLogitsLoss criterion, and Adam
               optimizer. Total parameter count: approximately 1.2M (vs. 1.1M for pure Transformer).

                7.8   Discussion and Implications
                7.8.1   When Do CNNs Help?
                The ablation study (Table 15) reveals a clear pattern: CNNs only help on high-fidelity tem-
                poral data (ESTA) and actively hurt on coarse aggregates (Kaggle/Combined).
                   ESTA Performance (+0.8pp): Player-level telemetry enables detection of local patterns:
                   • Multi-round win streaks (e.g., winning 3 consecutive rounds often predicts strong economy
                     for round 4)
                   • Eco-reset cycles (teams alternate full-buy, eco, full-buy in predictable 2-3 round windows)
                   • Equipment-saving strategies (losing with rifles saved → next round economic advantage)
                   The CNN’s 3-round receptive field (kernel size 3) aligns well with these 2-4 round temporal
                patterns, explaining the modest performance gain.
                   Kaggle Performance (-0.7pp): Economy-only features lack fine temporal structure:
                   • Equipment values aggregated at team level obscure player-specific save/force decisions
                   • Missing combat statistics (kills/deaths/utility) remove key momentum signals
                   • Temporal patterns are dominated by noise rather than signal, causing CNN overfitting
                   Strategic Implication: For esports analytics, convolutional layers should only be deployed
                when input data has fine-grained temporal structure. Coarse aggregates require simpler architec-
                tures to avoid overfitting.
                7.8.2   WhyDoes the CNN-Transformer Fail?
                The 44% failure/overfit rate represents a critical flaw. Suspected root causes:
                  1. Initialization Sensitivity: Kaiming initialization assumes ReLU nonlinearities and specific
                     variance properties.  Interaction between CNN ReLU activations and downstream Trans-
                     former LayerNorm may violate these assumptions for unlucky initialization draws.
                  2. Learning Rate Mismatch: CNN layers and Transformer layers may require different opti-
                                                                          −3
                     mal learning rates. The global learning rate of 1×10    maybetoohighfor CNN parameters
                     or too low for Transformer attention weights depending on initialization.
                  3. Gradient Flow Disruption: Convolutional layers compress sequence representations via lo-
                     cal receptive fields, potentially creating gradient bottlenecks. When backpropagating through
                     CNN → Projection → Transformer, gradients may vanish (failed runs) or explode (overfit
                     runs) despite gradient clipping.
                                                                                                          Page 34
              CSCE768 Final Project                                                           Jackie Wang
                 4. Architectural Mismatch: The CNN expects locally smooth features (neighboring rounds
                    have similar statistics), but CS:GO rounds exhibit high variance (eco rounds interrupt se-
                    quences). This mismatch may cause CNN filters to oscillate during training.
                  Future work should investigate:
                  • Layer-wise learning rates (e.g., lower LR for CNN, higher for Transformer)
                  • Alternative normalization schemes (GroupNorm or BatchNorm instead of LayerNorm)
                  • Residual connections around CNN layers to preserve gradient flow
                  • Systematic hyperparameter grid search (20+ seeds per configuration)
              7.8.3   Missing Component: Positional Encoding
              As noted in Section 7.1.1, neither the pure Transformer nor CNN-Transformer includes
              explicit positional encodings. This represents a fundamental architectural deficiency for CS:GO
              prediction.
